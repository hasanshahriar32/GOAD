\chapter{Game-Theoretic Autonomous Defense and NP-Hardness of Edge-Severing}
\label{ch:game_theory}

\section{From Passive Auditing to Active Autonomous Defense}
While detection and risk prioritization represent the initial imperatives of cybersecurity operations, real-world Security Operations Centers (SOCs) are overwhelmed by alert fatigue. In modern cloud and hybrid identity environments, where automated offensive frameworks (such as Certipy and BloodHound) execute lateral movement and domain compromise in under 30 seconds, passive auditing is fundamentally insufficient. Modern enterprise resilience demands \textbf{Autonomous Cyber Defense (ACD)}---systems capable of dynamically reconfiguring the identity topology to neutralize attack paths in real time.

However, transitioning from passive GNN classification to active autonomous defense introduces severe operational and theoretical dilemmas:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Business Availability Constraint:} An autonomous agent could trivially achieve $100\%$ security by deleting all security groups, severing all domain trusts, and revoking all certificate issuance templates. While this eliminates all attack paths, it inflicts catastrophic denial-of-service on legitimate business workflows.
    \item \textbf{Strategic Adversary Adaptation:} Attackers do not follow static paths; when an edge is blocked, intelligent adversaries dynamically adapt, pivoting to alternative delegation chains, trust relationships, or unmonitored service accounts.
    \item \textbf{Combinatorial Optimization Complexity:} Finding the minimal set of access control modifications that severs all attack paths while minimizing operational disruption is a combinatorial optimization challenge over massive graphs.
\end{enumerate}

To resolve these challenges, this chapter formalizes enterprise identity defense as a \textbf{Bayesian Stackelberg Security Game}, establishes the \textbf{NP-Hardness of Minimal-Capacity Edge-Severing (Theorem~2)}, specifies polynomial-time heuristic approximation algorithms, and proves convergence stability using the \textbf{ODE Method of Stochastic Approximation}.

\section{The Stackelberg Security Game Formulation}
We model the strategic interaction between an autonomous defender and an intelligent adversary on the Active Directory multigraph $G = (V, E)$ as a two-player, asymmetric, extensive-form \textbf{Stackelberg Security Game}.

\subsection{Players and Asymmetric Action Spaces}
The game features two rational, utility-maximizing players with asymmetric roles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Defender (Leader):} The enterprise defender acts first by committing to a mitigation policy $\pi_D$. The defender's action space $\mathcal{A}_D$ consists of choosing a subset of directed authorization edges to revoke, modify, or sever:
    \begin{equation}
    a_D = E_{\text{cut}} \subseteq E
    \end{equation}
    Each candidate edge $e \in E$ is associated with a strictly positive operational business disruption cost $c(e) \in \mathbb{R}^+$, representing the administrative friction, workflow interruption, or business impact incurred by revoking that permission.
    
    \item \textbf{The Attacker (Follower):} The adversary observes the post-mitigation graph $G' = (V, E \setminus E_{\text{cut}})$ through active directory queries. The attacker's action space $\mathcal{A}_A$ consists of selecting an optimal directed path:
    \begin{equation}
    a_A = \text{Path}(s \rightsquigarrow t) = (e_1, e_2, \dots, e_m)
    \end{equation}
    connecting an initial compromised low-privileged principal $s \in S_{\text{low-priv}}$ to a high-value Tier-0 administrative target $t \in T_{\text{high-value}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multi-Objective Utility Functions and Regularization}
To prevent the defender from executing destructive network disconnections, we formulate an asymmetric multi-objective utility function balancing risk reduction against operational availability:
\begin{equation}
U_D(a_D, a_A) = R_{\text{sec}}(G') - \lambda_{\text{ops}} \sum_{e \in E_{\text{cut}}} c(e) - \frac{\gamma_{\text{reg}}}{2} \|p_D\|_2^2 - \beta \cdot \sum_{(s, t) \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}} \mathbb{I}(t \in \operatorname{Reachable}(s, G'))
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $R_{\text{sec}}(G') \in [0, 1]$ is the continuous security reward proportional to the reduction in global attack surface blast radius computed via CertGraph node embeddings.
    \item $\sum_{e \in E_{\text{cut}}} c(e)$ is the linear operational disruption penalty quantifying business workflow friction.
    \item $\frac{\gamma_{\text{reg}}}{2} \|p_D\|_2^2$ ($\gamma_{\text{reg}} > 0$) is a strictly convex quadratic regularization penalty on the defender's edge-interdiction probabilities $p_D \in [0, 1]^{|E|}$. While the raw operational disruption cost is linear in discrete edge indicators, this quadratic term ensures strict concavity of the continuous relaxation objective with respect to the defender's mixed action $p_D$, guaranteeing a unique, Lipschitz-continuous best response.
    \item $\beta \gg 1$ is an operational penalty applied for each forbidden compromise pair that remains reachable.
    \item $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function returning $1$ if target $t$ remains reachable from $s$.
\end{enumerate}

The attacker's utility is the direct strategic dual:
\begin{equation}
U_A(a_D, a_A) = \sum_{t \in T} V_{\text{target}}(t) \cdot \mathbb{I}(t \in \operatorname{Reachable}(S, G')) - \sum_{e_i \in a_A} c_{\text{stealth}}(e_i)
\end{equation}
where $V_{\text{target}}(t)$ is the asset value of compromising Tier-0, and $c_{\text{stealth}}(e_i)$ represents the risk of detection associated with traversing edge $e_i$, grounded in deceptive Active Directory defense models where traversing deceptive edges triggers detection alarms or maximizes incident response times~\cite{ngo2023honeypot,ngo2024decoy}.

\section[Theoretical Complexity: NP-Hardness of Edge-Severing]{Theoretical Complexity: The NP-Hardness of Multi-Principal Access Interdiction}
A foundational question in autonomous cyber defense is whether an automated agent can compute the optimal, minimal-disruption edge-severing strategy in polynomial time. While severing paths between a single source and a single target is solvable via classical max-flow min-cut algorithms, enterprise identity environments require severing paths across multiple independent source-target pairs while preserving legitimate operational workflows. We formalize this challenge through the \textbf{Multi-Principal Access Interdiction (MPAI)} problem.

\begin{theorem}[NP-Hardness of Multi-Principal Access Interdiction]
\label{thm:nphardness}
Given an Active Directory multigraph $G = (V, E)$, non-negative operational disruption edge costs $c: E \to \mathbb{R}^+$, and a set of $k \ge 3$ forbidden source-target compromise pairs $\mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}} = \{(s_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^k$, finding the minimum-capacity edge cut $E_{\text{cut}} \subseteq E$ whose removal severs all directed paths for every $(s_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}$:
\begin{equation}
\min_{E_{\text{cut}} \subseteq E} \sum_{e \in E_{\text{cut}}} c(e) \quad \text{subject to } \forall (s_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}, \, \operatorname{Reachable}(s_i, t_i, G \setminus E_{\text{cut}}) = \emptyset
\end{equation}
is NP-hard.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\textit{Proof Outline.} The full, rigorous reduction is presented in Appendix~\ref{proof:theorem2}. We establish NP-hardness via a polynomial-time reduction from the classical \textbf{Directed Multiway Cut} problem, proven NP-complete by Garg, Vazirani, and Yannakakis~\cite{garg1994multiway}. It is critical to distinguish this result from prior Active Directory interdiction hardness literature: Guo et al.~\cite{guo2022practical,guo2023scalable} studied \textit{shortest-path} edge interdiction targeting a single destination (e.g., Domain Admin), establishing that maximizing the attacker's shortest path is W[1]-hard (even though complete severance of a single target is solvable in polynomial time via classical $(S, T)$ min-cut). In contrast, Theorem~\ref{thm:nphardness} formalizes \textit{Multi-Principal Access Interdiction (MPAI)}, proving that complete reachability interdiction across $k \ge 3$ independent forbidden compromise pairs is NP-hard even on directed identity topologies. Given an arbitrary instance of Directed Multiway Cut with directed graph $H = (V_H, E_H)$, terminals $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$, and capacity function $w: E_H \to \mathbb{R}^+$, we construct an Active Directory identity multigraph $G$ in polynomial time $\mathcal{O}(|V_H| + |E_H|)$. We demonstrate that an edge cut $E_{\text{cut}}$ severs all directed paths between forbidden principal-target pairs $\mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}$ if and only if the corresponding edge cut in $H$ separates all terminal pairs in $X$.

\begin{corollary}[Intractability of Brute-Force Remediation]
Because Multi-Principal Access Interdiction is NP-hard, exact combinatorial optimization algorithms (such as Integer Linear Programming or branch-and-bound search) exhibit exponential worst-case time complexity $\mathcal{O}(2^{|E|})$. For enterprise directory graphs containing hundreds of thousands of access control edges, computing exact optimal defenses is intractable.
\end{corollary}

\section{Polynomial-Time Heuristic Approximation Algorithms}
Because exact optimization is NP-hard, autonomous defense systems require polynomial-time approximation heuristics. We design a greedy capacity-disruption edge-severing heuristic (Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy_sever}).

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Greedy Capacity-Disruption Edge Severing Heuristic}
\label{alg:greedy_sever}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Active Directory Graph $G = (V, E)$; Forbidden pairs $\mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}$; Disruption cost function $c: E \to \mathbb{R}^+$; Maximum budget $B_{\text{ops}}$.
\Ensure Reconfigured graph $G' = (V, E \setminus E_{\text{cut}})$ with severed attack paths.
\State Initialize edge cut set $E_{\text{cut}} \gets \emptyset$, cumulative disruption $C_{\text{total}} \gets 0$
\While{$\exists (s, t) \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}} \text{ with path } \mathcal{P} \text{ from } s \text{ to } t \text{ in } G$}
    \State Compute path centrality across all active paths: $\forall e \in E, \, \phi(e) \gets \sum_{\mathcal{P} \in \Pi(\mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}})} \mathbb{I}(e \in \mathcal{P})$
    \State Compute cost-efficiency ratio for each candidate edge:
    \State \quad $\rho(e) \gets \frac{\phi(e)}{c(e)}$
    \State Identify optimal candidate: $e^* \gets \operatorname{argmax}_{e \in E \setminus E_{\text{cut}}} \rho(e)$
    \If{$C_{\text{total}} + c(e^*) > B_{\text{ops}}$}
        \State \textbf{break} (Budget constraint reached)
    \EndIf
    \State Sever edge: $E \gets E \setminus \{e^*\}$
    \State Update sets: $E_{\text{cut}} \gets E_{\text{cut}} \cup \{e^*\}$, $C_{\text{total}} \gets C_{\text{total}} + c(e^*)$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $G' = (V, E), E_{\text{cut}}, C_{\text{total}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Approximation Dynamics and Centrality}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy_sever} iteratively computes the path centrality $\phi(e)$ (the number of unprivileged attack paths traversing edge $e$) and evaluates the cost-efficiency ratio $\rho(e) = \phi(e) / c(e)$. By prioritizing edges that participate in the largest number of attack trajectories while incurring minimal operational disruption, Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy_sever} provides an efficient polynomial-time greedy heuristic ($\mathcal{O}(|E| \cdot (|V| + |E|))$). While the optimal Directed Multiway Cut admits an $\mathcal{O}(\log |\mathcal{P}_{\text{forbidden}}|)$ LP-rounding approximation~\cite{garg1994multiway}, greedy edge-centrality interdiction is strongly motivated by practical Active Directory security studies (Ward, 2021~\cite{ward2021algorithms}; Zhang et al., 2023~\cite{zhang2023scalable}; Goel et al., 2024~\cite{goel2024hardening}), where tree-like identity structures allow greedy centrality heuristics and policy-search methods to achieve near-optimal path severance within practical execution budgets.

\section{Convergence Dynamics via Two-Timescale Stochastic Approximation}
To train the autonomous agent within the continuous state space provided by CertGraph node embeddings, we deploy multi-agent reinforcement learning.

The stability of co-adaptive reinforcement learning (where both attacker and defender policies update simultaneously) is prone to non-stationarity. We formalize policy convergence using the \textbf{Two-Timescale Stochastic Approximation Framework}~\cite{borkar2008stochastic}.

\subsection{Two-Timescale Step-Size Conditions}
Let $\theta_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_D}$ denote the parameter vector of the defender's policy network, and let $\phi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_A}$ denote the parameter vector of the attacker's policy network at discrete training step $k$. The coupled parameter updates follow:
\begin{align}
\theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k + \alpha_k \left[ \nabla_\theta U_D(\theta_k, \phi_k) + M_{k+1}^{(D)} \right] \\
\phi_{k+1} &= \phi_k + \eta_k \left[ \nabla_\phi U_A(\theta_k, \phi_k) + M_{k+1}^{(A)} \right]
\end{align}
where $M_{k+1}^{(D)}$ and $M_{k+1}^{(A)}$ are zero-mean Martingale difference noise terms.

The step-size schedules satisfy the two-timescale separation condition~\cite{borkar2008stochastic}:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha_k = \infty, \quad \sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha_k^2 < \infty, \quad \sum_{k=1}^\infty \eta_k = \infty, \quad \sum_{k=1}^\infty \eta_k^2 < \infty, \quad \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{\alpha_k}{\eta_k} = 0
\end{equation}
Because $\alpha_k / \eta_k \to 0$, the attacker's policy updates on a faster timescale, asymptotically tracking the unique best response $\phi^*(\theta)$ to the defender's quasi-static policy $\theta$.

\subsection{Continuous Limit and Stackelberg Equilibrium Convergence}
In general multi-agent games, simultaneous joint gradient updates ($\dot{\theta} = \nabla_\theta U_D, \dot{\phi} = \nabla_\phi U_A$) do not form a conservative gradient flow and frequently exhibit limit cycles or rotational divergence. The two-timescale stochastic approximation schedule~\cite{borkar2008stochastic} fundamentally resolves this issue by enforcing $\lim_{k \to \infty} \alpha_k / \eta_k = 0$. The follower updates rapidly relative to the leader, tracking the unique best-response manifold $\phi^*(\theta)$. Decoupled from non-stationary follower dynamics, the slow-timescale defender parameter trajectory $\{\theta_k\}$ asymptotically tracks the projected ordinary differential equation:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\theta}(t) = \nabla_\theta U_D(\theta(t), \phi^*(\theta(t)))
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Almost Sure Convergence to Local Stackelberg Equilibrium]
With strictly convex quadratic regularization $\frac{\gamma_{\text{reg}}}{2} \|p_D\|_2^2$ ($\gamma_{\text{reg}} > 0$), the defender's continuous relaxation objective is strongly concave with respect to interdiction probabilities, ensuring a compact, Lipschitz continuous best-response manifold $\phi^*(\theta)$. Under the two-timescale step-size conditions, the parameter sequence $\{\theta_k, \phi_k\}$ converges almost surely to a locally asymptotically stable Stackelberg equilibrium $(\theta^*, \phi^*(\theta^*))$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Stackelberg MARL Agent Architecture and Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:marl_architecture}
To ensure reproducibility and empirical rigor, we formalize the exact neural network architectures and hyperparameter configurations governing the multi-agent reinforcement learning optimization:

\paragraph{Defender Policy Network ($\pi_\theta$):}
The defender's policy network evaluates candidate edges $e = (u, v) \in E$ for autonomous revocation. The input representation for edge $e$ concatenates the endpoint node embeddings generated by CertGraph's $L = 2$ heterogeneous GNN backbone with the one-hot relation type vector:
\begin{equation}
x_e = \left[ h_u^{(L)} \,\|\, h_v^{(L)} \,\|\, \mathbf{1}_{\tau(e)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{64 + 64 + 8} = \mathbb{R}^{136}
\end{equation}
The edge embedding $x_e$ is processed by a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with layer dimensions $[136 \to 128 \to 64 \to 1]$. Each hidden layer applies Layer Normalization followed by LeakyReLU activations ($\alpha = 0.01$) and a dropout rate of $0.1$. The scalar output is passed through a Sigmoid activation to yield the unconstrained interdiction probability $\tilde{p}_D(e) \in [0, 1]$. To enforce differentiability during backpropagation while strictly respecting the operational budget $B_{\text{ops}}$, we apply Gumbel-Softmax relaxation across candidate edge scores during training, transitioning to deterministic top-$B_{\text{ops}}$ edge selection during inference.

\paragraph{Attacker Policy Network ($\pi_\phi$):}
The simulated adversary employs a Deep Q-Network (DQN) architecture to learn optimal lateral movement traversal. Given the current compromised principal node state $s_t = v$, the Q-network evaluates all outgoing authorization edges $a = (v, w) \in \mathcal{N}_{\text{out}}(v)$. The state-action value $Q_\phi(s, a)$ is computed by passing neighbor representations through a 2-layer Relational Graph Convolutional Network (R-GCN) with hidden dimension $64$, followed by a linear projection head. Exploration follows an $\epsilon$-greedy schedule with exponential decay.

\subsection{Comparative Defense Baselines}
We compare four remediation strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Random Revocation (Measured Baseline):} Selects and revokes authorization edges uniformly at random until reaching the disruption budget $B_{\text{ops}}$.
    \item \textbf{Degree-Centrality Revocation (Measured Baseline):} Greedily revokes edges incident to nodes possessing the highest total degree normalized by operational disruption cost.
    \item \textbf{Greedy Capacity-Disruption Heuristic (Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy_sever}) (Static / Myopic Baseline):} Computes path centrality $\phi_{G_0}(e)$ statically on the unhardened initial graph $G_0$ and severs edges maximizing $\rho(e) = \phi_{G_0}(e) / c(e)$ without co-adapting to follower rerouting.
    \item \textbf{Stackelberg Co-Adaptive Policy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:stackelberg_marl}):} Two-timescale reinforcement learning policy coupled with the Backward Causal DAG Minimal-Cut Interdiction Engine, dynamically tracking adversarial lateral movement paths to compute the optimal edge-revocation cut under disruption budget $B_{\text{ops}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Analysis of Operational Findings}
\label{sec:defense_findings}
The empirical results compiled in Table~\ref{tab:game_simulation_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:game_sim} confirm three core operational insights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Measured Superiority of Co-Adaptive Stackelberg Interdiction:} The Stackelberg Co-Adaptive Policy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:stackelberg_marl}) integrated with the Backward Causal DAG Minimal-Cut Interdiction Engine systematically and strictly outperforms the Static Greedy heuristic across all disruption budgets. At $B_{\text{ops}} = 6$, Stackelberg severs $72.8 \pm 16.2\%$ of attack paths compared to $65.6 \pm 16.1\%$ for Static Greedy (paired $t(29) = 4.00, p = 4.01 \times 10^{-4}$; Wilcoxon signed-rank $W = 20.0, p = 9.02 \times 10^{-4}$). At $B_{\text{ops}} = 12$, Stackelberg reaches $86.4 \pm 9.8\%$ vs. $82.9 \pm 9.8\%$ for Greedy ($p = 0.0486$), reaching $91.8 \pm 5.6\%$ at $B_{\text{ops}} = 18$ ($p = 0.0284$) and $92.9 \pm 4.0\%$ at $B_{\text{ops}} = 24$ ($p = 0.0277$).
    \item \textbf{Operational Immutability Plateau and the Runtime Defense Residual:} In real enterprise networks, approximately $8\%$ of permissions represent mission-critical operational dependencies ($E_b \subsetneq E$) that IT administrators refuse to revoke. Consequently, proactive graph interdiction alone plateaus at $92.9 \pm 4.0\%$ path neutralization at $B_{\text{ops}}=24$ (vs. $91.7 \pm 6.6\%$ for Static Greedy). This proves a fundamental thesis insight: \textit{proactive graph hardening alone can never achieve 100\% security without breaking production dependencies}. The resulting $\sim 7\%$ residual attack surface strictly necessitates the runtime provenance monitoring provided by CIPG.
    \item \textbf{Administrative Efficiency and Disruption Reduction:} Crucially, the Stackelberg co-adaptive policy achieves superior path interdiction while inflicting dramatically less operational friction on enterprise workflows. At maximum budget ($B_{\text{ops}} = 24$), Stackelberg revokes an average of only $\mathbf{6.2 \pm 2.0}$ edges, whereas Static Greedy requires $\mathbf{10.8 \pm 0.9}$ edges---representing a \textbf{$42.6\%$ reduction in administrative disruption}. By co-adapting to the attacker's best-response rerouting at each step, Stackelberg targets downstream bottleneck chokepoints (\texttt{Group} $\to$ \texttt{Template} \texttt{Enroll}, \texttt{WriteDacl}, and \texttt{Template} $\to$ Tier-0 escalation links) without wasting budget on high-cost upstream \texttt{MemberOf} group memberships.
\end{enumerate}
