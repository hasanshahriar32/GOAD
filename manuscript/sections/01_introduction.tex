\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern enterprise networks rely almost universally on Microsoft Active Directory (AD) to administer centralized identity, policy enforcement, and authentication services. Within this ecosystem, Active Directory Certificate Services (ADCS) operates as the enterprise Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), providing automated enrollment, cryptographic identity certificates, and Kerberos public-key pre-authentication (PKINIT, RFC 4556~\cite{rfc4556}) for millions of principals. Despite its foundational operational role, ADCS constitutes one of the most critical and exploited attack surfaces in contemporary cybersecurity. Research formalized by SpecterOps in 2021~\cite{schroeder2021certified} systematized certificate-service escalation, with subsequent work extending the ESC taxonomy, wherein subtle misconfigurations in certificate templates, access control lists (ACLs), and Certificate Authority (CA) registration flags allow low-privileged domain principals to compromise entire domain forests.

\subsection{The Multi-Faceted Vulnerability Landscape of ADCS}
In an ADCS deployment, certificate issuance is governed by Active Directory objects known as \textit{Certificate Templates} (stored in the LDAP configuration naming context). When an enterprise template exposes an insecure combination of attributes—such as the \texttt{CT\_FLAG\_ENROLLEE\_SUPPLIES\_SUBJECT} bitflag alongside an Extended Key Usage (EKU) permitting Client Authentication (\texttt{1.3.6.1.5.5.7.3.2}) or Smart Card Logon (\texttt{1.3.6.1.5.2.3.4}), without requiring CA administrator approval—any domain principal granted enrollment permissions can request an X.509 certificate specifying an arbitrary Subject Alternative Name (SAN). The attacker can supply the user principal name (UPN) of a Domain Administrator (e.g., \texttt{Administrator@domain.local}), obtain a cryptographically valid certificate signed by the enterprise root CA, and submit that certificate to the Kerberos Key Distribution Center (KDC) via PKINIT to request a Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT), when the KDC accepts the resulting identity mapping. Certificate-binding policy and patch level can prevent this outcome~\cite{microsoft2025strongbinding}.

While static privilege audit tools such as BloodHound~\cite{robbins2017bloodhound} and Certipy~\cite{lyak2021certipy} have gained widespread industrial adoption, defending enterprise ADCS infrastructure against sophisticated adversaries reveals three structural failure modes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inductive Shortcut Memorization in Static GNNs:} Recent academic efforts have applied Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and graph interdiction algorithms to automate vulnerability discovery and privilege defense over enterprise identity multigraphs~\cite{guo2022practical,king2023euler,goel2024optimizing,guo2024limited,zhang2025rethinking,ngo2024catch}. However, we discover that standard GNN formulations (GCN, GAT, and additive-skip architectures) succumb to severe \textit{inductive shortcut collapse}. Rather than learning genuine multi-hop structural reachability from unprivileged principals through intermediate security groups to enrollment endpoints, the message-passing layers memorize localized template bitflags. When evaluated against adversarial \textit{hard negatives} (configurations that possess dangerous template flags but are structurally unreachable due to enrollment restrictions or issuance requirements), the evaluated GNN baselines obtain $0.00\%$ accuracy ($0/63$) on this deliberately constructed benchmark; production alert rates are not measured by those cases.
    
    \item \textbf{Static Snapshot Blindness to Temporal Mutation Attacks:} Under vectors such as ESC4 (template ACL abuse) and ESC9 (certificate templates omitting the security extension), an attacker with \texttt{WriteOwner} or \texttt{GenericAll} permissions dynamically mutates a benign certificate template, issues an illegitimate administrative certificate, and immediately rolls the template attributes back to their pristine baseline. The tested configuration projection returns to its baseline value after rollback; this does not establish equality of the entire directory database. A scanner restricted to those restored configuration attributes cannot distinguish this history from a history with no intervening mutation. This observation does not assign an unconditional zero recall to tools that also inspect issuance history or other evidence.
    
    \item \textbf{The CA--KDC Logging Discontinuity and SAN Concealment:} Even when SOCs deploy advanced enterprise identity telemetry (e.g., whole-network logon-session provenance~\cite{liu2026hades,liu2026cpd}), a certificate-level investigation can still lack the fields needed for exact linkage: the selected certificate-identifier field projections of the captured CA ($\pi_{\text{CA}}$) and KDC ($\pi_{\text{KDC}}$) events are disjoint ($\pi_{\text{CA}} \cap \pi_{\text{KDC}} = \emptyset$). Certificate Authority logs (Event IDs 4886 and 4887) record the template name and Request ID, while KDC PKINIT logs (Event ID 4768) record only the certificate serial number and SHA-1 thumbprint. Furthermore, when an attacker submits an enrollee-specified SAN inside the raw PKCS\#10 ASN.1 binary CSR (\texttt{szOID\_ENROLL\_CERTTYPE\_EXTENSION}), the CA security event log suppresses the requested SAN string entirely. SAN-based CA/KDC correlation already exists in practical queries~\cite{insecurity2023adcs,splunk2026esc1}; the narrower challenge here is defensible certificate attribution when that evidence is missing, contradictory, or late.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
To address these fundamental challenges, this paper presents \textbf{CertGraph}, an integrated static-to-dynamic neuro-symbolic framework that bridges topological access-control reasoning, cryptographic audit provenance, and game-theoretic interdiction. The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis of Static Model Assumptions and Shortcut Behavior:} We retain the static architecture and formalize a conditional intrinsic-feature sensitivity bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem1}). An augmentation-matched audit checks 6,000 historical-implementation predictions and corrects reachable deep-path labels. A separate literal two-layer bilinear realization checks 10,800 held-out predictions on family-disjoint inputs, with matched initialization and augmentation. The historical gate reduces false positives while missing out-of-horizon positives. The literal bilinear and additive models have identical classifications in the new study; both miss deep reachable positives. These results motivate explicit symbolic treatment of unresolved cases rather than a universal shortcut-cure claim.
    
    \item \textbf{Cryptographic Invariant Provenance Graph (CIPG):} We connect directory mutation, issuer-scoped CA records, and successful KDC certificate authentication while distinguishing definite, possible, conflicting, and withdrawn lineage explanations. A frozen 5,400-case synthetic confirmation shows a coverage/certainty trade-off: 55.82\% definitive link coverage and 99.06\% possible-set coverage on its randomized subset, with 107 correct and zero false definite mutation overlaps. Four final 12-trial matrices in Windows 2016 and an independent Windows 2019 forest anchor 42 successful exact-certificate authentications and six rejections to public certificate, CA and KDC evidence. Current-patch request-form controls distinguish UPN-only rejection from SID-URL administrative use after restored configuration, with matched signed requests within each form and actual Certipy/Sigma comparisons. Its inconsistent audit-clock model motivates a separate operation-order reconstruction using the recorded synchronous capture sequence. Fault-budget and order-aware component studies expose both gains and failure conditions; these do not change the static model architecture.
    
    \item \textbf{Game-Theoretic Interdiction under Assigned Disruption Costs:} We formulate the \textbf{Disruption-Budgeted Stackelberg Remediation Game} between an enterprise defender and an optimal cost-minimizing attacker. We prove that Multi-Principal Access Interdiction (MPAI) is NP-complete via reduction from Directed Multiway Cut (Theorem~\ref{thm:nphardness}). Evaluated across 30 generated identity graphs under realistic operational constraints ($E_b \subsetneq E$), our co-adaptive Stackelberg policy achieves higher reported mean interdiction than static greedy on the evaluated synthetic topologies, with budget-specific significance results, neutralizing \textbf{$98.5 \pm 4.0\%$} of attack paths at $B_{\text{ops}}=24$ (vs. $97.6 \pm 6.5\%$ for greedy) while revoking \textbf{$6.0$ vs. $10.9$ edges}—a \textbf{$45.0\%$ reduction in revoked-edge count} under the assigned disruption costs. The reported residual is specific to the evaluated policy and instances. A new 60-case exhaustive audit exposes heuristic optimality gaps. A path-preserving projection retains all 325 queried pairs at 5,000 actual nodes and gives a measured median $7.54\times$ ratio across fifteen timing pairs, including projection cost; the historical larger ratio used different query tasks.

\end{itemize}
